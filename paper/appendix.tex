\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

Throughout, the hash functions and the stream are fixed, and probabilities are over the record
priorities. The anchor $c^\star$ of a query is a function of the counters, which are determined by the
stream and the hash functions; it does not depend on the priorities, so conditioning on it introduces no
selection bias.

\subsection{Pair tables}

\begin{proposition}[Pair tables]\label{prop:pair}
Let $q$ be a two-predicate query on attributes $a<b$, and let the table of $(a,b)$ use a pairwise
independent hash function into $w_2$ cells. In expectation over that hash function, the linearised
variance of the calibrated estimator (Eq.~\pref{eq:cal}) with the pair cell as anchor is at most
$\bigl(\tfrac1\rho-1\bigr)\min\bigl\{f(q),\,(N-f(q))/w_2\bigr\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $Z=n_c-f$ be the number of records with a different value pair that collide with the query's pair.
By Proposition~\pref{prop:calvar} the variance is $(1/\rho-1)\,fZ/(f+Z)$, which is concave in $Z$ and at
most $(1/\rho-1)\min(f,Z)$. Pairwise independence gives $\E Z\le(N-f)/w_2$, and Jensen's inequality
gives $\E[fZ/(f+Z)]\le f\,\E Z/(f+\E Z)\le\min(f,\E Z)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\pref{thm:calbound}}

Write $n=n_{c^\star}$, $f=f(q)$, $R=f/n$, and $y_r=[r\in q]$ for $r\in c^\star$. Let $I_r$ indicate that
record $r$ is sampled; $\hat f_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{r\in c^\star}I_ry_r/\pi_r$ and
$W=\sum_{r\in c^\star}I_r/\pi_r$ (records outside $c^\star$ do not match $q$ and do not enter $W$).

\paragraph{(a) Equal rates.} Let $\pi_r=\rho$ and let $k=\sum_{r\in c^\star}I_r$ and $X=\sum_rI_ry_r$.
Then $\hat f_{\mathrm C}=nX/k$ if $k\ge1$ and $0$ otherwise. The indicators are i.i.d., so conditionally
on $k$ the sampled records of $c^\star$ form a uniformly random $k$-subset, and $X$ is hypergeometric with
$\E[X\mid k]=kf/n$. Hence $\E[\hat f_{\mathrm C}\mid k]=f$ for every $k\ge1$ and
$\E\hat f_{\mathrm C}=f\Pr[k\ge1]=f\bigl(1-(1-\rho)^n\bigr)$, so the bias is $-f(1-\rho)^n\in[-fe^{-\rho n},0]$.
Hoeffding's inequality holds for sampling without replacement~\cite[Thm.~4]{hoeffding}, so
$\Pr[|X/k-R|>\epsilon\mid k]\le2e^{-2k\epsilon^2}$. Since $|\hat f_{\mathrm C}-f|=n|X/k-R|$,
\[
 \Pr\bigl[|\hat f_{\mathrm C}-f|>\epsilon n\bigr]\le\Pr[k<\rho n/2]+\max_{k\ge\rho n/2}2e^{-2k\epsilon^2},
\]
which is at most $e^{-\rho n/8}+2e^{-\epsilon^2\rho n}$,
using the multiplicative Chernoff bound $\Pr[k<(1-\frac12)\E k]\le e^{-\E k/8}$ for the binomial $k$
(the event $k=0$ is included). For a uniform sample of fixed size $M$ out of $N$ (a reservoir), the sampled
records of $c^\star$ are again, conditionally on $k$, a uniform $k$-subset, and $k$ is hypergeometric,
for which the same Chernoff bound holds~\cite{hoeffding}; so (a) and Corollary~\pref{cor:samples} hold
with $\rho=M/N$.

\paragraph{Corollary~\pref{cor:samples}.} If $\rho n\ge\ln(3/\delta)/\epsilon^2$ then
$2e^{-\epsilon^2\rho n}\le2\delta/3$, and if moreover $\epsilon\le1/3$, then $\rho n\ge9\ln(3/\delta)\ge
8\ln(3/\delta)$ and $e^{-\rho n/8}\le\delta/3$.

\paragraph{(b) Unequal rates.} Because $\sum_{r\in c^\star}(y_r-R)=0$,
\[
 \hat f_{\mathrm C}-f=\frac nW\bigl(\hat f_{\mathrm{HT}}-RW\bigr)=\frac nW\,D,\qquad
 D=\sum_{r\in c^\star}\Bigl(\frac{I_r}{\pi_r}-1\Bigr)(y_r-R).
\]
The summands of $D$ are independent, have mean zero and are bounded in absolute value by
$\max(1/\pi_r-1,1)\cdot\max(R,1-R)\le1/\rho$, and
$\Var D=\sum_r(1/\pi_r-1)(y_r-R)^2\le(1/\rho-1)\sum_r(y_r-R)^2=(1/\rho-1)f(1-R)=:v$. Bernstein's
inequality~\cite[Thm.~2.10]{concentration} gives $\Pr[|D|\ge t]\le2\exp\bigl(-t^2/(2(v+t/(3\rho)))\bigr)$,
and $t=\sqrt{2vL}+\frac23L/\rho$ with $L=\ln(2/\delta)$ satisfies $t^2\ge2L(v+t/(3\rho))$, so
$|D|<\sqrt{2vL}+\frac23L/\rho$ with probability at least $1-\delta$. For the denominator, $W$ is a sum of
independent non-negative terms with $\E W=n$ and $\sum_r\E[(I_r/\pi_r)^2]=\sum_r1/\pi_r\le n/\rho$;
Maurer's inequality~\cite{maurer} gives $\Pr[W\le n-t]\le\exp(-t^2\rho/(2n))$, i.e.,
$\Pr[W\le n/2]\le e^{-\rho n/8}$. On the intersection of both events, $|\hat f_{\mathrm C}-f|\le2|D|$,
which is the claim.

\paragraph{(c) Collisions.} Let $c^\star$ be the pair cell (or any lighter cell addressed by $q$) and
$Z=n-f$. Then $f(1-f/n)=fZ/(f+Z)\le\min(f,Z)$. Every record whose value pair differs from the query's
collides with probability at most $1/w_2$, so $\E Z\le(N-f)/w_2$, and by Markov's inequality
$Z\le(N-f)/(\delta'w_2)$ with probability at least $1-\delta'$ over the hash function. Substituting in (b)
and taking a union bound proves (c).

\paragraph{Remarks.} The analysis assumes Poisson sampling at thresholds that do not depend on the
priorities. For \cs, whose threshold $B$ is chosen by the budget control, Theorem~\pref{thm:unbiased}
shows that the HT sums $\hat f_{\mathrm{HT}}$ and $W$ remain unbiased, but the tail bound is stated for
fixed thresholds only. For conjunctions with more predicates than the order of the tables, $n$ can be much
larger than $f$, and the bound is additive in $n$; Cormode, Dickens and Woodruff~\cite{projfreq} show that
for column subsets revealed after the data, many projected-frequency problems need space exponential in
the number of columns, so a guarantee relative to $f$ for every subset is out of reach in polynomial
space. Our guarantee is relative to $f$ exactly where an exact (up to collisions) table addresses all of
the query's attributes.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\pref{thm:hold}}

\paragraph{Idealised process.} Give every row $r$ of the stream an i.i.d.\ uniform priority $u_r$ and let
$\mathcal D(u)$ be the deterministic process that performs exactly the operations of \csh, with every
priority it needs read from $u$: when a row of a tuple without an entry arrives, its priority is compared
with the tuple's current threshold; counted rows are not inspected; re-anchoring an entry whose start
priority is no longer below the threshold $\tau$ moves the start to the first later counted row with
priority below $\tau$, or drops the entry if there is none. The implementation draws the number of skipped
rows from a geometric distribution with parameter $\tau$ and the new start priority uniformly from
$[0,\tau)$; since the rows after a start were never inspected, these are exactly the conditional
distributions of the corresponding quantities of $\mathcal D(u)$ given everything inspected so far
(principle of deferred decisions). Hence the state of the implementation at any time has the same
distribution as that of $\mathcal D(u)$, and it suffices to analyse $\mathcal D$.

\paragraph{Notation.} Thresholds only decrease: $\nstar_t$ is non-decreasing in time and $B$
non-increasing, so $\tau_t(T)=\min(1,B(T)/\nstar_t(T))$ is non-increasing. Let $\tau_t$ be the final
threshold and $S_t=\{r\in t:u_r<\tau_t\}$. After the final resolution at query time, tuple $t$ holds an
entry iff $S_t\ne\emptyset$, its start is the first row of $S_t$, and its count $c_t$ is the number of rows
of $t$ from the start on: every row of $t$ before the start was compared, at its arrival or during a
re-anchoring, with a threshold that is at least $\tau_t$ and found not below it.

\paragraph{Per-row substitution.} Fix a row $r$ of tuple $t$ and all priorities except $u_r$. Let
$\mathcal D^0$ be the run with $u_r$ replaced by $0$, and $\tau^0_t$ the final threshold of $t$ in
$\mathcal D^0$, a function of $u_{-r}$ only. We show: $u_r<\tau^0_t$ iff $r\in S_t$, and in that case both
runs have the same trajectory of $B$, so $\tau_t=\tau^0_t$.

Suppose $u_r<\tau^0_t$ and consider the two runs event by event. Before $r$ arrives they coincide. We
maintain the invariant that both runs have the same $B$, the same counters and the same entries with the
same counts and starts, and that the start priorities coincide except that the entry of $t$ may start at
$r$, with priority $0$ in $\mathcal D^0$ and $u_r$ in $\mathcal D(u)$. Because $\tau_t(T)\ge\tau^0_t>u_r$ in
$\mathcal D^0$ at every time $T$, and the runs agree on $\tau_t(T)$ as long as the invariant holds, (i) when
$r$ arrives and $t$ holds no entry, $r$ opens one in both runs; (ii) whenever an entry of $t$ is
re-anchored, $r$ qualifies as a start in both runs (and the earlier rows qualify identically); (iii) an
entry starting at $r$ is never re-anchored in either run. It remains to show that every selection returns
the same $B$. A compaction computes $z_e=u_{\mathrm{start}(e)}\nstar_e$ for all entries and sets
$B'=\min(B,\,\text{the }(K{+}1)\text{-st smallest }z_e)$. The multisets of $z$-values are $\{0\}\cup Z$ in
$\mathcal D^0$ and $\{z_r\}\cup Z$ in $\mathcal D(u)$, where $z_r=u_r\nstar_t$ if the entry of $t$ starts at
$r$ (otherwise they are equal). In $\mathcal D^0$ the $(K{+}1)$-st smallest value is the $K$-th smallest
of $Z$, and since $u_r<\tau^0_t\le B'^{\,0}/\nstar_t$ we have $z_r<B'^{\,0}\le$ the $K$-th smallest of $Z$; so
$z_r$ is also among the $K$ smallest values in $\mathcal D(u)$, whose $(K{+}1)$-st smallest is again the
$K$-th smallest of $Z$. Both runs obtain the same $B'$, re-anchor the same entries in the same way (by
(ii) and (iii)), and repeat the selection the same number of times. Insertions of other tuples depend only
on $B$ and the counters. By induction the invariant holds until query time, so $\tau_t=\tau^0_t>u_r$ and
$r\in S_t$.

Conversely, suppose $r\in S_t$, i.e., $u_r<\tau_t$. The same induction with the roles of the runs exchanged
applies: now $u_r<\tau_t\le\tau_t(T)$ in $\mathcal D(u)$ at all times, and at a selection in $\mathcal D(u)$
with result $B'$ we have $z_r<B'$; if $z_r$ were not below the $(K{+}1)$-st smallest value $q$ of
$\{z_r\}\cup Z$, then $B'\le q\le z_r$, a contradiction; so $z_r$ is among the $K$ smallest, both
$(K{+}1)$-st smallest values equal the $K$-th smallest of $Z$, and the runs coincide. Hence
$\tau^0_t=\tau_t>u_r$. (Ties among $z$-values have probability zero.)

\paragraph{Unbiasedness of the row-level estimate.} Since $u_r$ is uniform and independent of $u_{-r}$,
$\Pr[r\in S_t\mid u_{-r}]=\Pr[u_r<\tau^0_t\mid u_{-r}]=\tau^0_t$, and $\tau_t=\tau^0_t$ on that event.
Therefore $\E[\mathbf 1_{r\in S_t}/\tau_t\mid u_{-r}]=1$, and $H=\sum_ty_t|S_t|/\tau_t$ satisfies
$\E H=\sum_ty_tm_t$.

\paragraph{Rao--Blackwellisation.} Let $\mathcal F$ be the information revealed by the implementation up
to query time (all comparisons it made, the start priorities and the final state). Given $\mathcal F$, the
$c_t-1$ rows after the start of a held tuple were never inspected, so their priorities are i.i.d.\
uniform and independent of $\tau_t$, which is $\mathcal F$-measurable; the start is in $S_t$; and all
earlier rows of $t$, and all rows of a tuple without an entry, are known to lie outside $S_t$. Hence
$\E[|S_t|\mid\mathcal F]=1+(c_t-1)\tau_t$ if $t$ holds an entry and $0$ otherwise, i.e.,
$\E[|S_t|/\tau_t\mid\mathcal F]=X_t$. By the tower property $\E\sum_ty_tX_t=\E H=\sum_ty_tm_t$, and by the
law of total variance $\Var\sum_ty_tX_t\le\Var H$.

\paragraph{Variance for a fixed schedule.} If the thresholds do not depend on the priorities, the rows of
$t$ are in $S_t$ independently with probability $\tau=\tau_t$; with $J$ the index of the first such row,
$X_t=m_t-J+1/\tau$ if $J\le m_t$ and $0$ otherwise, and a direct computation gives $\E X_t=m_t$ and
$\Var X_t=(1-\tau)\bigl(1-(1-\tau)^{m_t}\bigr)/\tau^2=\E\bigl[(1-\tau)\mathbf 1_{\text{entry}}/\tau^2\bigr]$.
The $X_t$ of different tuples are independent, so $\sum_ty_t^2(1-\tau_t)\mathbf 1_{\text{entry}}/\tau_t^2$
is an unbiased estimate of $\Var\sum_ty_tX_t$. Under budget control we use it as a plug-in estimate.
\qed

\subsection{Propositions of Section~\pref{sec:anatomy}}

\paragraph{Proposition~\pref{prop:hoeffding}.} $|S\cap q|/m$ is a mean of $m$ indicators with expectation
$f(q)/N$; apply Hoeffding's inequality~\cite{hoeffding} (which holds for sampling without replacement) and
a union bound over $\mathcal Q$. For conjunctions of equalities over $p$ attributes with at most $D$ values
each, $\ln|\mathcal Q|\le p\ln(D+1)$.

\paragraph{Observation~\pref{obs:min}.} A record is in $\bigcap_cS_c$ iff it is in every $R_c$ and its hash
is at most every $\tau_c$.

\paragraph{Proposition~\pref{prop:rate}.} The reservoir holds $p\,d\,w\,B\,\beta/(32p)$ rows; compare its
rate with $\rho_O(q)=B/n_{\max}(q)$. Both synopses then estimate $f(q)$ with variance approximately
$f(q)(1-\rho)/\rho$, so a lower rate translates directly into a larger error.

\paragraph{Proposition~\pref{prop:bias}.} A record lands in all cells of $C(q)$ iff, for every predicate it
violates, it collides with the query value in all $d$ rows, which has probability $w^{-d}$ per violated
predicate, independently. For a two-predicate query on values with marginal frequencies $f_1,f_2$,
$N_1=f_1+f_2-2f(q)$.

\subsection{Small-count intervals}

Under Poisson sampling at fixed thresholds every matching record $r$ is retained with probability
$\pi_r\ge\pi_q$ (Section~\pref{sec:ci}), so $\Pr[m=0]=\prod_{r\in q}(1-\pi_r)\le e^{-\pi_qf}$. If
$f>U=\ln(1/\alpha)/\pi_q$, the probability of observing $m=0$ is below $\alpha$, so reporting $[0,U]$
when $m=0$ errs with probability at most $\alpha$ for every $f$. For $m\ge1$, the calibrated estimate is a
weighted sum of (approximately Poisson) indicators, and the gamma interval of Fay and
Feuer~\cite{fayfeuer} for such sums uses the estimate $y$, its variance estimate $v$ and the largest weight
$w_M$: the lower limit is the $\alpha/2$ quantile of a gamma distribution with mean $y$ and variance $v$,
the upper limit the $1-\alpha/2$ quantile of one with mean $y+w_M$ and variance $v+w_M^2$. We use
$w_M=(n_{c^\star}/W_{c^\star})/\pi_q$, the calibrated weight of one more matching record at the smallest
rate, and truncate to the hard bounds $[m,n_{c^\star}]$.

\paragraph{Implementations.} (a)~The Java sources of~\cite{omni} (commit \texttt{8dc67b5} of the
authors' repository \texttt{omnisketch}) are compiled unmodified (JDK~21) and driven by a harness of about 230 lines that
replaces only their input/output layer: it reads our binary streams and query files, obtains the sketch
parameters from their \texttt{RamToPar} and \texttt{DetermineB} classes, and calls their
\texttt{OmniSketch.add()} and \texttt{query()}. (b)~A C++ port reproduces (a) bit by bit: the
hash functions based on \texttt{java.util.Random}, the parameter search, the tree-set bookkeeping, both
estimators and the memory accounting. (c)~Our optimised implementation uses 63-bit hashes, bounded binary
heaps in flat arrays instead of red-black trees, a dense array of per-cell thresholds so that a rejected
update costs one comparison per cell, and sorted cells so that intersections are linear merges. (d)~The
configuration search uses (c) with oracle accounting, which is deliberately more favourable to \omni than
any deployable configuration, over the nine shapes $(8,3)$, $(28,3)$, $(64,2)$, $(256,2)$, $(1024,2)$,
$(256,3)$, $(1024,1)$, $(4096,1)$ and $(4096,2)$ of $(w,d)$.

\paragraph{The port is exact.} For \NumValRuns{} runs---\NumValStreams{} streams, budgets of 1, 10, 50 and
100\,MB, three repetitions---we ran the Java code and the port on the same records and the same
2\,000--2\,400 queries. All \NumValQueries{} intersection sizes, Theorem~3.3 estimates and scaled
estimates coincide, as do the selected parameters $(w,d,B,b)$ and the reported memory usage; test vectors
of the two hash functions are compared separately. In the paper, ``\omni (authors' code)'' is the port
with the scaled estimator.

\paragraph{The code reproduces the published error levels.} Figure~6 of~\cite{omni} reports the error of
three-predicate queries on the synthetic streams: about $10^{-5}$ for Zipf with $\alpha=1$,
$1.3\cdot10^{-4}$ for $\alpha=1.3$ and $2.6\cdot10^{-4}$ for $\alpha=1.5$ at 50--200\,MB, and close to
zero for the uniform stream. On streams from the authors' generator (Zipf$^\dagger$: value $k$ has rank
$k$) and for our three-predicate record-sampled queries, their code yields $\NumFigSixZipfTenTen$,
$\NumFigSixZipfTenFifty$ and $\NumFigSixZipfTenHundred$ ($\alpha=1$; 10, 50 and 100\,MB),
$\NumFigSixZipfThirteenTen$, $\NumFigSixZipfThirteenFifty$ and $\NumFigSixZipfThirteenHundred$
($\alpha=1.3$), $\NumFigSixZipfFifteenTen$, $\NumFigSixZipfFifteenFifty$ and
$\NumFigSixZipfFifteenHundred$ ($\alpha=1.5$), and $\NumFigSixUnifTen$ on the uniform stream: the same
order, the same levels within a factor of about 1.5, and the same lack of improvement beyond 50\,MB.

\paragraph{R1: the returned estimate.} The method \texttt{query()} implements Eq.~\pref{eq:thm33}: it
returns the bound $2n_{\max}L/(B\epsilon^2)$ whenever $|S_\cap|<3L/\epsilon^2$ and stores the always-scaled
value $\lceil|S_\cap|\,n_{\max}/B\rceil$ in a separate field (\texttt{case2Estimate}). With
$\epsilon=\delta=0.1$ the switch-over point lies between about 2\,600 and 3\,600 sampled records for all
budgets we use, more than half of $B$ at 10\,MB. The bound is therefore returned for
\NumValBoundFracLo--\NumValBoundFracHi\% of the multi-predicate queries at every budget from 1 to
100\,MB, and the returned values are off by $\NumJavaThmNaeLo$--$\NumJavaThmNaeHi$ times the stream size
(column ``Thm.~3.3'' of Table~\ref{tab:validation}). The analysis notebooks shipped with the journal
version's repository compute the plotted ``observed error'' from \texttt{case2Estimate}, and the journal
version's code always returns the scaled estimate. Anyone who benchmarks against the public code through
its \texttt{query()} interface will measure the first column; we use the scaled estimator, the favourable
choice.

\paragraph{R2: the cell hash.} A value $v$ is mapped to its $d$ cells by re-seeding a
\texttt{java.util.Random} with $v$ plus the row number and drawing $d$ integers below $w$; the same function
is used for all attributes. The first output of this linear congruential generator is, up to a bit mask,
$\lfloor w\cdot\mathrm{frac}(a\,v/2^{48})\rfloor$ with $a/2^{48}\approx9\cdot10^{-5}$: for $w=8$ the
first-row cell changes only once every $\approx1\,400$ consecutive integers. With the authors' generator
the frequent Zipf values are $1,2,3,\dots$ and share one first-row cell, which holds up to
\NumRowOneShareZipfTen\%, \NumRowOneShareZipfThirteen\% and \NumRowOneShareZipfFifteen\% of the
Zipf$^\dagger$ streams with $\alpha=1.0$, $1.3$ and $1.5$, and \NumRowOneShareTaxi\% of Taxi and MAWI (with
our random permutation of the Zipf values: \NumRowOneShareOurZipfTen\% for $\alpha=1$). Since the usable
rate is $B/n_{\max}$, this costs accuracy: on Zipf$^\dagger$ our implementation with the same $(w,d,B)$ is
\NumJavaOverOursZipfDagLo--\NumJavaOverOursZipfDagHi$\times$ more accurate than the authors' code (10\,MB
and more); on our permuted streams the two are within a factor \NumJavaOverOursZipfHi. On dictionary-coded
attributes with few values, by contrast, the second and third rows act as nearly perfect hash functions:
on Taxi the authors' code is \NumOursOverJavaTaxiLo--\NumOursOverJavaTaxiHi$\times$ \emph{more} accurate
than ours; on MAWI the ratio is \NumJavaOverOursMawiLo--\NumJavaOverOursMawiHi. Neither implementation is
uniformly better, which is why the paper always includes the authors' code and takes the best variant.

\paragraph{R3: hash width and bookkeeping.} The number of hash bits is
$b=\lceil\ln(4B^{2.5}/\delta)\rceil$ (natural logarithm), 20--31 bits for budgets of 1--100\,MB, and
record identifiers are mapped to $\{0,\dots,\min(2^b,2^{31}-1)-1\}$, so distinct records share hash values.
A cell stores a set of distinct hash values, counts a duplicate arrival towards its capacity while filling
up, and evicts its maximum without inserting when a duplicate arrives later, so cells end up with fewer
than $B$ values (at 10\,MB the sketch uses \NumJavaUsedTen\,MB of the nominal \NumJavaNominalTen\,MB). This
explains the collapse of the authors' code below 5\,MB.

\paragraph{R4: the synthetic generator.} In the public snapshot the method that creates the synthetic
streams overwrites its exponent argument with $\alpha=1.5$, so that all four ``distributions'' of the
sensitivity experiment would be generated identically. We call the underlying Zipf generator directly.

The authors of the journal version~\cite{omni-vldbj} report that \omni outperforms adaptive
Sample-and-Hold (aSH)~\cite{ash}. We cannot rerun its bounded-deletion experiments within our scope, but
its code (repository \texttt{wiegerpunter/omni-deletes}, commit \texttt{17b969e}), configuration files and
query generator allow us to examine the insert-only case.

\emph{Accounting.} The baseline keeps one entry per distinct full tuple and is charged $32p+384$ bits per
entry: the tuple, five 64-bit fields and two pointers; a reservoir row is charged $64p+32$ bits. For $p=9$
an aSH entry costs $2.3$ times a reservoir row of $32p$ bits; \omni is charged 31 bits per stored hash. On
streams whose tuples are almost all distinct, aSH is a uniform sample with noisier weights, so the
accounting alone costs it a factor $1.5$--$1.8$ in standard error. We port the baseline (adaptive
Sample-and-Hold keyed on the full tuple, 10\% ingest buffer, batched ejection) and report it under that
accounting and under a lean one ($32p+64$ bits per entry).

\emph{Insert-only results.} Table~\ref{tab:journal} uses the stream and configuration of that code's
insert-only setting. In normalized absolute error, aSH under the journal accounting is
\NumJSOmniVsAshLo--\NumJSOmniVsAshHi$\times$ more accurate than \omni in the journal configuration on the
journal-style queries (\NumJROmniVsAshLo--\NumJROmniVsAshHi$\times$ on our record-sampled queries); a
reservoir charged $32p$ bits per row is \NumJSOmniVsResLo--\NumJSOmniVsResHi$\times$ more accurate, and
the calibrated sample another \NumJSCalTwoVsResLo--\NumJSCalTwoVsResHi$\times$.

\emph{Workload and metric.} The journal-style procedure draws, for each of 300 sampled records, one query
for every $k=1,\dots,p$. On a stream with nine attributes most of these queries match a single record
(Table~\ref{tab:journalk}): the mean true answer falls from about $1.5\cdot10^5$ for $k=1$ to
\NumJKTruthHigh{} for $k\ge6$, and \NumJKShareHigh\% of the queries have five or more predicates. For such
queries the metric decides the winner. Under Bernoulli sampling at rate $\rho$, an unbiased estimate of a
count $f=1$ is $1/\rho$ with probability $\rho$ and $0$ otherwise, so its expected \emph{relative} error
is $2(1-\rho)\approx2$, whereas an estimator that returns $0$ has relative error $1$. For $k\le4$, aSH
under the journal accounting has a \NumJKAshAbsLo--\NumJKAshAbsHi$\times$ lower absolute error than \omni
and the reservoir a \NumJKResAbsLo--\NumJKResAbsHi$\times$ lower one. For $k\ge6$ all methods are off by
one or two records; \omni, whose intersections are empty for \NumJKOmniZeroHigh\% of these queries, has a
mean relative error of \NumJKOmniRelHigh, aSH of \NumJKAshRelHigh{} and the reservoir of
\NumJKResRelHigh. Averaged over the workload, the mean relative error at 10\,MB is \NumJSMreOmni{} for
\omni, \NumJSMreAsh{} for aSH and \NumJSMreRes{} for the reservoir. A relative-error average over a
workload dominated by near-empty conjunctions therefore ranks \omni above the sampling baseline, although
the baseline is more accurate on every query class whose answer is large enough to be estimated. Whether
this is the mechanism behind the published comparison we cannot tell from the code alone; what the code
shows is that, in the insert-only setting, neither the accounting nor the algorithm of the sampling
baseline makes it less accurate than \omni in absolute terms.

\paragraph{Setup details.} Taxi: vendor, passenger count, rate code, pick-up and drop-off zone, payment
type, hour, weekday, and binned distance, fare, tip and duration. MAWI: 2010-01-01, samplepoint~F.
DMV: record type, registration class, city, state, ZIP code, county, model year, make, body type, fuel type,
registration date, colour and three indicator flags, in file order (the vehicle identification number is
dropped). The journal-style experiment uses 9 Zipf$^\dagger$ attributes with $\alpha=1.3$ and queries
generated as that version's code does. Hydra's sketch seeds are fixed in its code, so it has a single
repetition. \textsc{USS+C$i$} and \textsc{Coco+C$i$} add the counter rows (and pair and triple tables)
to USS and CocoSketch and estimate with Eq.~\pref{eq:cal}; ``\csh (C3)'' is the hold mode with pair and
triple tables ($\phi_2=\phi_3=0.15$). The allocation exponent $\gamma$ replaces $n$ by $n^\gamma$ in
Eq.~\pref{eq:nstar}, interpolating between equal allocation per stratum ($\gamma=1$) and uniform
sampling ($\gamma=0$). Timings ran on one performance core (16\,GB RAM, Linux, GCC~16.2).

\paragraph{Table share.} The NAE is flat in the share of the pair tables between $\phi_2=0.1$ and $0.5$
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), as the analysis of Section~\pref{sec:cal} predicts; triple tables help when
queries with three and more predicates matter.

\paragraph{House weight and allocation.} Without a house stratum ($\lambda=0$) rare anchors on Taxi are
answered almost exactly but frequent queries suffer; $\lambda=1$ gives up some of the rare-anchor gain for
frequent-query accuracy close to that of the uniform sample; $\lambda=1/4$ is a reasonable alternative when
rare values matter most. A sub-linear allocation exponent $\gamma<1$ without a house stratum is dominated by
$\gamma=1$ with a house stratum. On MAWI none of these choices matters much for rare anchors.

\paragraph{Number of buckets.} On Taxi, rare-anchor accuracy improves until every value owns a bucket
($w\approx4096$) and is flat afterwards. The automatic rule (5\% of the budget for C1 counters) selects
$w=8192$ at 10\,MB.

\paragraph{Calibrating to all anchors.} Fitting the $2^k$ contingency table of a query to all available
marginals by iterative proportional fitting lowers the error by a further 15--45\% with exact marginals in a
prototype, but with hashed tables the marginals of collided cells enter as if they were exact, and the
result is unreliable (rows ``all anchors'': better in some cells, markedly worse in others, in particular
on MAWI). We therefore keep the single lightest anchor.

\paragraph{Unbiasedness checks.} On a small stream (20\,000 records, 500 stored rows, so that compactions
are frequent) and \NumProbeSeeds{} independent seeds, the HT estimate of the stream size deviates from the
truth by $\NumProbeCs\%\pm\NumProbeCsSe\%$ for \cs and $\NumProbeCsh\%\pm\NumProbeCshSe\%$ for \csh (mean
$\pm$ standard error); on a stream with at most 2\,500 distinct tuples and 200 entries, where held counts
and re-anchoring dominate, \csh deviates by $\NumProbeCshRep\%\pm\NumProbeCshRepSe\%$. On the real streams
at 1\,MB (0.5\,MB for Zipf), summed over the queries with $f(q)\ge1000$, the HT estimates of \cs deviate
by $\NumBiasHtTotalTaxi\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeTaxi\%$ (Taxi, \NumBiasSeedsTaxi{} seeds),
$\NumBiasHtTotalMawi\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeMawi\%$ (MAWI, \NumBiasSeedsMawi{} seeds) and
$\NumBiasHtTotalZipf\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeZipf\%$ (Zipf, $\alpha=1.5$), and those of \csh by
$\NumBiasHtTotalHoldTaxi\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeHoldTaxi\%$,
$\NumBiasHtTotalHoldMawi\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeHoldMawi\%$ and
$\NumBiasHtTotalHoldZipf\%\pm\NumBiasHtTotalSeHoldZipf\%$. A record's priority depends only on the seed and
the record identifier, so the seed ranges of the three streams are disjoint. The calibrated estimator, a
ratio estimator, deviates by $\NumBiasCalTotalTaxi\%\pm\NumBiasCalTotalSeTaxi\%$ on Taxi.
These checks are consistent with Theorems~\pref{thm:unbiased} and~\pref{thm:hold}.
